## GPT Image 2.6 prompting guide
Choose a model, tune complexity, and guide image edits.
##  Overview
Start with the image you need, then describe the subject, composition, style, and constraints. For edits, identify what should change and what must stay the same. Refine one thing at a time and inspect the result.
GPT Image 2.6 Flare is the smaller model for workflows where speed matters most. GPT Image 2.6 Sunburst is the base model for workflows that need more detail or precise edits. Both support image generation, editing, and transparent backgrounds.
For API setup and request examples, see the  image generation guide .
##  Choose a model
For a new workflow, start with  GPT Image 2.6 Flare  if response time matters most. Start with  GPT Image 2.6 Sunburst  if the image must meet demanding requirements for detail, layout, or edit accuracy.
Your current workflow
Start by testing
Your existing GPT Image 2 workflow meets your quality requirements, or you use GPT Image 2.5 Flare.
gpt-image-2.6-flare . Check quality and response time on the prompts you already use.
GPT Image 2 falls short on a complex task, or you use GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst.
gpt-image-2.6-sunburst . Check the details that determine whether you can use the image.
If Sunburst meets your requirements, try Flare with the same prompts, reference images, dimensions, and quality setting. Compare the results and response times on your workload.
##  Model parameters
Set these fields in the API request. Use the prompt to describe the image you want.
Parameter
GPT Image 2.6 settings
model
Use  gpt-image-2.6-flare  or  gpt-image-2.6-sunburst .
quality
Controls how much visual detail the model can render, such as texture or small lettering. Choose  auto ,  low ,  medium ,  high ,  xhigh , or  max .
complexity
Controls how much the model plans before rendering. Choose  low ,  medium , or  high . Both Sunburst and Flare use  high  when you omit the field.
size
Sets the output dimensions in pixels. Use  auto  or a supported size such as  1024x1024  (square),  1536x1024  (landscape), or  1024x1536  (portrait).
background
Use  auto ,  opaque , or  transparent . For transparent PNG or WebP, check the decoded alpha channel.  SVG output  also supports transparency.
output_format
Use  png ,  jpeg , or  webp . Generations also support   svg   and   gif  . Only JPEG and WebP support  output_compression .
markup
On edits, upload a transparent PNG overlay with the same pixel dimensions as the first input image. See  Guide an edit with markup .
For a complex layout, start with the default and check how the model placed labels and connected objects. To compare complexity levels, keep  quality  fixed. If the layout is correct but small text is hard to read, compare quality settings at the same complexity. See  Compare complexity .
For a custom  size , use multiples of 16 for both dimensions and an aspect ratio from 1:3 to 3:1. The landscape slide example uses  1536x864 .
Omit  input_fidelity  on image edits. GPT Image 2.6 does not support  reasoning.summary .
##  Migrate an existing workflow
-  Save a baseline.  Collect representative prompts, reference images, and outputs from your current model. Include difficult edits, exact text, product geometry, and transparent assets where relevant.
-  Test the corresponding model.  For a 2.5 Flare workflow, try 2.6 Flare first. For a 2.5 Sunburst workflow, try 2.6 Sunburst first. For a GPT Image 2 workflow, use the  model selection table .
-  Keep the first comparison consistent.  Use the same prompt, reference images, size, quality, and output format. Leave complexity at the model default.
-  Inspect the result.  Check text, layout, identity, and details that should survive an edit. For a sequence of edits, check the final image as well as each intermediate result.
-  Tune one setting at a time.  Change either quality or complexity. Repeat each request and compare how often the output meets your criteria and how long it takes.
-  Roll out by workflow.  Move a small share of traffic after the new model meets your criteria. Track response time, failures, retries, and cost per accepted image before you expand.
When migrating from GPT Image 1 or 1.5, use its reference tab to check parameter differences and shutdown dates. If part of an image must remain pixel-identical, composite the approved edit into the original.
##  Prompting fundamentals
-  Define the result.  Name the subject and intended use, such as a product photograph, advertisement, or diagram. Specify the composition, aspect ratio, and important placement constraints. For complex requests, organize the prompt as scene, subject, details, and constraints, using labeled sections.
-  Choose a maintainable format.  Short prompts, descriptive paragraphs, JSON-like structures, instructions, and tags can all express the same intent. Choose the format that makes the requirements easiest to read and update rather than relying on special syntax.
-  Describe visible details.  Name materials, lighting, colors, and the visual medium. Request “photorealistic” or “real photograph” explicitly when that is the goal, and describe framing and texture. Treat camera specifications as cues for appearance, not a guarantee of exact physical simulation. For wide, cinematic, low-light, rainy, or neon scenes, specify scale, atmosphere, and color instead of relying on mood words alone.
-  Specify people and actions.  Describe body framing, relative scale, gaze, and interaction with objects. Instructions such as “full body visible, feet included,” “looking down at the open book,” or “hands naturally gripping the handlebars” make the intended pose and action clearer.
-  Specify exact text.  Put required wording in quotes and describe its position and typography. Spell unusual words or brand names letter by letter when needed. Ask for no extra text, then check spelling and legibility in the output. Compare medium or high quality for small text, dense information, or multiple fonts.
-  Separate changes from constraints.  For edits, say “change only X” and list the details to preserve, such as identity, geometry, layout, lighting, or labels. State exclusions such as unwanted text, logos, or watermarks. For precise local edits, also identify saturation, contrast, arrows, camera angle, and surrounding objects that must remain unchanged.
-  Assign roles to references.  Identify each input by number and purpose: subject, style, clothing, or background. Explain how the inputs should combine and which elements should move where.
-  Iterate deliberately.  Pass the previous output as the next edit input, request one change, and repeat the details to preserve. References such as “same style as before” can carry context, but restate critical constraints if the result drifts. Compare results before adding more instructions.
Most examples omit  complexity  and use the model default. Adapt the prompts to your own images and requirements.

##  Generate SVG output
For a logo you can scale or recolor by editing its paths, send the  Field & Flour bakery logo prompt  to  POST /v1/images/generations . Set  output_format="svg"  and  n=1 . SVG also supports  background="transparent" , so the space around the mark can take the color of the surface behind it.
Omit  stream ,  partial_images , and  output_compression  from the SVG request. Decode  data[0].b64_json  from base64 and save the resulting XML as a  .svg  file.

##  Generate a looping GIF
For a smooth loop, give the subject a starting pose to return to and say whether the camera should move.
A little monkey in an olive-green hooded coat stands in a snowy pine forest at dusk. A wooden cabin glows in the background. The monkey holds a glowing amber paper lantern at chest height, slowly lifts it, looks up and blinks, then lowers it back to its starting pose. Keep the lantern in its hands and the camera still. Make the motion loop smoothly.
Send the prompt to  POST /v1/images/generations  with  output_format="gif" . This example uses  gpt-image-2.6-sunburst ,  size="512x512" ,  quality="high" , and the default  high  complexity. With  frame_count=9  and  frame_duration_ms=200 , the loop lasts 1.8 seconds. See  GIF settings and limits .
##  Control style and lighting
Describe a photograph through its subject, framing, light, and texture. This example specifies a candid composition and explicitly excludes heavy retouching.
The example uses  size="1024x1536" ,  quality="medium" .
Create a photorealistic candid photograph of an elderly sailor standing on a small fishing boat.
He has weathered skin with visible wrinkles, pores, and sun texture, and a few faded traditional sailor tattoos on his arms.

##  Overview
Start with the image you need, then describe the subject, composition, style, and constraints. For edits, identify what should change and what must stay the same. Refine one thing at a time and inspect the result.
GPT Image 2.5 includes two model choices. GPT Image 2.5 Flare is the small model, optimized for speed, with image quality comparable to GPT Image 2. GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst is the base model, optimized for quality, with higher image quality than GPT Image 2. Both models offer improvements in precise editing and subject preservation.
For API setup and request examples, see the  image generation guide .
##  Choose a model
For a new workflow, start with GPT Image 2.5 Flare when speed is the priority, or GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst when demanding quality requirements are the priority. Once the output meets your requirements, look for opportunities to reduce latency.
For migrating from a current image model, use your current image quality as the starting point. Both models support image generation, editing, and transparent backgrounds.
Your current workflow
Start by testing
An existing, validated GPT Image 2 workflow already meets your quality requirements
GPT Image 2.5 Flare. Check whether you can retain acceptable quality while reducing latency.
A complex use case where GPT Image 2 does not meet your quality requirements
GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst. First establish that it delivers the quality you need.
If GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst meets your quality requirements, then test GPT Image 2.5 Flare with the same prompts and inputs. Switch to GPT Image 2.5 Flare if it also meets those requirements and improves latency. Keep GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst when its quality advantage is necessary for your workflow.
Measure response time and quality on your own workload. Results depend on your prompts, reference images, output dimensions, and quality settings; a speed improvement on one workload doesn’t establish a fixed improvement on another.

##  Prompting fundamentals
-  Define the result.  Name the subject and intended use, such as a product photograph, advertisement, or diagram. Specify the composition, aspect ratio, and important placement constraints. For complex requests, organize the prompt as scene, subject, details, and constraints, using labeled sections.
-  Choose a maintainable format.  Short prompts, descriptive paragraphs, JSON-like structures, instructions, and tags can all express the same intent. Choose the format that makes the requirements easiest to read and update rather than relying on special syntax.
-  Describe visible details.  Name materials, lighting, colors, and the visual medium. Request “photorealistic” or “real photograph” explicitly when that is the goal, and describe framing and texture. Treat camera specifications as cues for appearance, not a guarantee of exact physical simulation. For wide, cinematic, low-light, rainy, or neon scenes, specify scale, atmosphere, and color instead of relying on mood words alone.
-  Specify people and actions.  Describe body framing, relative scale, gaze, and interaction with objects. Instructions such as “full body visible, feet included,” “looking down at the open book,” or “hands naturally gripping the handlebars” make the intended pose and action clearer.
-  Specify exact text.  Put required wording in quotes and describe its position and typography. Spell unusual words or brand names letter by letter when needed. Ask for no extra text, then check spelling and legibility in the output. Compare medium or high quality for small text, dense information, or multiple fonts.
-  Separate changes from constraints.  For edits, say “change only X” and list the details to preserve, such as identity, geometry, layout, lighting, or labels. State exclusions such as unwanted text, logos, or watermarks. For precise local edits, also identify saturation, contrast, arrows, camera angle, and surrounding objects that must remain unchanged.
-  Assign roles to references.  Identify each input by number and purpose: subject, style, clothing, or background. Explain how the inputs should combine and which elements should move where.
-  Iterate deliberately.  Pass the previous output as the next edit input, request one change, and repeat the details to preserve. References such as “same style as before” can carry context, but restate critical constraints if the result drifts. Compare results before adding more instructions.
